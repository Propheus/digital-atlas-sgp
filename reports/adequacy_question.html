An Essay on Urban Adequacy

The Adequacy Question

Singapore's HDB towns pass the binary test of self-containment. They fail it the moment we ask how much, not whether.

Singapore  ·  12 May 2026  ·  v0.2
✦   ✦   ✦

In the founding decades of Singapore's public housing programme, the Housing & Development Board committed to a deceptively radical idea: each new town would be a complete settlement. A resident could live, work, learn, eat, see a doctor, attend church, find a friend, and grow old — all without leaving its boundary. Self-containment was not architectural sentimentality; it was infrastructure policy, social engineering, and a hedge against CBD congestion all at once.1

Half a century later, the 23 HDB towns have together absorbed almost 3.5 million residents.2 They are the most studied and most replicated urban form on earth. The doctrine, it is widely believed, has held.

This essay asks a smaller, more inconvenient question: at what level of resolution is that belief true?

§ IThe doctrine, and the test

The reference stack is not invented for this essay; it is reconstructed from HDB and URA planning standards.3 Twelve amenity types are present, in some configuration, in every textbook description of a Singapore town: a hawker centre, an MRT or LRT station, primary and secondary schools, preschool, a GP clinic, a pharmacy, a supermarket, a library, a sports facility, a park, and a community club.

The audit's first move is procedural and deliberately blunt. For each town, count whether at least one instance of each amenity exists within the planning area boundary. A town with all twelve scores 12 / 12. The doctrine, so framed, holds when the score is high.

It holds. Of 23 towns, 21 score 10 of 12 or above.

Hougang scores a perfect twelve. Thirteen others sit at eleven; eight at ten. Only Yishun lands at nine, and even that result is brittle — one of its missing amenities is "MRT/LRT station," which Yishun very obviously possesses.4 The data attribution, not the town, is what breaks.

If a planner in 1975 wished to know whether her doctrine had survived to 2025, this is the chart she would be shown. She would, reasonably, be pleased.

Figure 1. The doctrine appears to hold. All 23 HDB towns cluster between 9 and 12 of 12 on the binary stack-presence test. The dashed line marks the 10/12 "passing" threshold.

§ IILook closer

Now widen the question. Bedok and Sembawang are both ticked "yes" for hawker centres. Bedok has 23 of them. Sembawang has 3. They serve 274,650 and 113,810 residents respectively.2 Per hundred thousand residents, Bedok's hawker provision is roughly seven times Sembawang's. Both pass the test.

Repeat the exercise across the other eleven amenity types and a different picture emerges. The doctrine, recast as adequacy, is less a settled fact and more a question of which town one happens to live in.

§ IIIThree symptoms of the same condition

i. The hawker bequest

Mature central estates — Geylang, Kallang, Bukit Merah, Queenstown — were planned around their hawker centres. Bukit Merah holds 14 of them in 12 km². The post-1990s towns were planned around kopitiams and mall food courts, with hawker centres treated as optional civic furniture rather than baseline infrastructure. Choa Chu Kang and Sembawang each hold three.5 The doctrine survives in the binary checklist; the underlying infrastructural philosophy did not.

ii. The library scarcity

Of the twelve amenity types, only one is genuinely thin everywhere. Nine of twenty-three towns register zero places coded as a library. The remainder hold one or two. Either Singapore's NLB town libraries are systematically under-coded in the place catalogue, or libraries genuinely do sit at the precinct edge of the towns they serve. Both possibilities deserve the field visit they will get in v0.3.

iii. The Sengkang anomaly

Sengkang houses 268,040 residents. The catalogue records three GP clinics in its boundary — a per-capita rate of 1.1 per 100,000, the lowest in Singapore by a factor of nearly three. Geylang, of comparable build vintage at its core, holds 31. This is almost certainly a data-attribution artefact (CHAS-network clinics in mall complexes assigned to neighbouring planning areas), but it is also the kind of artefact a binary stack-presence test cannot detect. If the test cannot tell three from thirty-one, what exactly is it testing?

§ IVThe mature estates hold the depth

Run the per-capita exercise across all twelve amenity types and a structural pattern resolves. The first-generation HDB towns — Toa Payoh (1965), Bukit Merah (1960s), Queenstown (1953), Bedok (1970s), Ang Mo Kio (1973) — concentrate amenity depth that the second- and third-generation towns simply do not. They also concentrate Singapore's elderly: Bedok alone houses 62,660 residents aged 65 and over.2

The newer towns — Punggol, Sengkang, Sembawang, Bukit Panjang, Choa Chu Kang — consistently hit the binary checklist while sitting in the bottom quartile on per-capita rates for hawker centres, GP clinics, sports facilities, and libraries. That this is the exact set the v0.2 audit flags as zero-hawker is not coincidence; it is the same finding viewed through a different lens.6

One reading is benign: amenity build-out follows the population it serves on a lag; the new towns will fill in. Another is structural: the planning doctrine itself shifted in the 1990s away from publicly-built civic infrastructure (NEA hawker centres, NLB libraries, SportSG town stadia) toward private-substitute equivalents (Koufu food courts, mall retail, condominium gyms). On the binary stack-presence test these substitutions register as a pass. On any per-capita or affordability-weighted test, they do not.

§ VIWhat adequacy would actually mean

If the binary stack-presence test understates the picture, the per-capita test in §V improves it but does not finish it. Three further moves are needed before adequacy can be claimed in any rigorous sense.

Walk distance, not boundary inclusion

An amenity counted "in" a town may be a twenty-five-minute walk from where most of its residents actually live. The next iteration of this audit, computed against the atlas's 214,000-node OSM walking graph, will report the share of each town's residents within 400m, 800m, and 1.2km of each amenity instance — the more honest version of "adequacy" for a population in which one in five is over 65.

Demographic weighting

A clinic per 100,000 residents is not the same metric in Punggol — where 9.8% of residents are 65 or over — as in Outram, where 27.0% are. Adequacy is not a constant. It is a function of the population whose adequacy is in question.

Substitution accounting

The hawker-centre count in Punggol is zero. The cooked-food provision is not. A v1.0 extension to the companion Aging-Hawker Gap report adds Kopitiam (87 outlets), Kimly (44), Koufu (25), Toast Box and Ya Kun — the chain-precinct-coffeeshop infrastructure that absorbed the hawker function in post-1990s towns. With substitutes counted, Punggol's effective rate rises to 4.7 stall-equivalents per thousand elderly. With substitutes counted, Choa Chu Kang's rate is still 2.1. Substitution helps. It does not equalise.

The numbers in this essay are anchored against verifiable sources; the interpretations are not. Three honest data limitations should colour every claim above.

Place taxonomy. The atlas's "Library" tag is materially under-populated against NLB's actual ~28 town libraries. The "MRT/LRT Station" tag misses Yishun NS13. The "GP Clinic" tag almost certainly under-counts mall-tenant CHAS clinics. v0.3 reconciles each against authoritative source registries.

Boundary attribution. Amenities at planning-area edges are assigned by point-in-polygon, which under-counts cases where, for example, Tampines Round Market sits within Tampines East boundary. Anomalies in the per-capita columns above (Sengkang GP, Yishun MRT, Tampines hawker) deserve the manual checking they will receive.

Walk distance is the missing axis. Every per-capita number above is a town-internal aggregate. Where, within the town, the resident lives matters more than the per-capita rate. The walk-graph iteration cannot come too soon.

§ VIICoda — what the doctrine asked of itself

The HDB self-containment doctrine of the 1970s did not promise that every town would hold an MRT station. It promised that residents would not have to leave the town for everyday life. Whether that promise survives in 2026 depends on what is meant by the word have.

If have to leave means "must travel out for medical treatment, schooling, or food," the doctrine holds. Every HDB town clears that threshold — the binary stack is intact almost everywhere.

If it means "may live a daily life of equivalent texture and depth as a resident of the older estates," the doctrine is straining. The mature central towns hold infrastructural depth that the post-1990s towns substitute for with private-sector equivalents. The substitutes work, but they work differently. They cost more. They lack the third-place social function. And they lend themselves less to the kind of egalitarian, intergenerational town-life that the original doctrine took as its objective.

This is not an argument for nostalgia. It is an argument for a measurement framework that can detect the difference. The next version of this audit will, on the walking graph, on the demographic weighting, and on the extended substitution stack, test whether that promise still holds — not at the boundary, but at the doorstep.

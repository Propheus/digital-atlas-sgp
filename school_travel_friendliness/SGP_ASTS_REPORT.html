Key terms, in plain language

This study chains six methods: schools → walking network → space syntax → catchment → entropy index → Geographic Detector. Here is what each one means, the data behind it, and why it matters for planning.

01

Schoolswhere every journey ends

The government primary schools, each pinned to its exact location. Everything in the study is measured in the area around these points — they are the anchor of the analysis.

In this study160 of Singapore's ~179 MOE primary schools (a few dropped as data duplicates); spread West 43 · North-East 42 · Central 28 · North 28 · East 27.
For plannersMOE's Primary-1 registration gives priority to homes within 1 km (then 1–2 km), so the area right around each school is where most of its pupils actually live — and could walk.
↓
02

Walking networkthe web of footpaths

A digital map of every footway, sidewalk, park path and pedestrian crossing, stitched into one connected graph the computer can “walk” along. Built from OpenStreetMap.

In this study170,121 nodes (junctions) and 463,880 edges (path segments) covering all of Singapore.
For plannersThis is the real surface children travel on. Distances are measured along paths, not straight lines — so a canal or expressway blocking a route is correctly counted as a detour, the way a child experiences it.
↓
03

Space syntaxhow readable the streets are

An established urban-morphology science that scores a street layout on two things — Integration (how easy a place is to reach from everywhere else) and Choice (how likely a street is to lie on a natural through-route). Simple, well-integrated networks are easy to navigate; tangled ones are not.

In this studyComputed with the cityseer engine on a 235,600-node “dual” model of the network, at 800 m and 1600 m walking radii.
For plannersA legible network is one a 7-year-old can follow without getting lost, and where people and traffic move predictably. It is a structural wayfinding-and-safety measure, independent of how nice any single street looks.
↓
04

Catchmentthe active school travel space

The bundle of streets a child can reach within 1 km of walking along the network from the school gate — not a 1 km circle on a map, but the real reachable area. This is the “Active School Travel Space” the study evaluates.

In this study160 catchments; on average ~31 km of walkable streets over ~0.9 km² each, from compact (3.8 km of streets) to sprawling (66 km).
For plannersIt is drawn to match the MOE 1 km priority band exactly, so it is the precise area where an upgrade would change the most pupils' walk to school.
↓
05

Entropy indexone fair score from six measures

The six raw measures (Integration, Choice, crossings, signals, greenery, footpath density) are fused into a single 0–100 score by the entropy weight method — a statistical rule that gives more weight to indicators that genuinely vary and carry information, and less to flat ones. No official chooses the weights, so there is no agency bias.

In this studyData-derived weights: greenery 0.26 · choice 0.19 · signals 0.19 · integration 0.16 · crossings 0.12 · footpaths 0.08. Schools then split into Low / Medium / High thirds.
For plannersAn auditable, defensible score whose weights come from the data, not opinion — important for any public-facing prioritisation that has to withstand scrutiny.
↓
06

Geographic Detectorwhat explains the map

A spatial-statistics test that asks “how much of the friendliness pattern does each background factor explain?”, producing a power score q from 0 to 1. Its interaction mode checks whether two factors together explain more than either alone.

In this studySchool centrality q=0.22 (strongest) · population density 0.11 · district size 0.08 · transport convenience 0.06 — all positive. Every factor pair is “nonlinear-enhancing” (density × centrality reaches q=0.40).
For plannersIt reveals the levers: friendliness is not random — it tracks how centrally a school sits in the street network, amplified by density. That points to where structural change, not cosmetics, would help most.

A core–periphery pattern

Friendliness rises toward the newer planned towns of the west and north-east and falls in the dense central core — the inverse of 15-minute amenity access. It reflects the walking environment (greenery, legible street networks, controlled crossings), the same core–periphery signature the Lanzhou study reported.

Reading the scores — what friendliness means here

The score is a 0–100 relative ranking of how supportive each school's 1 km walking catchment is for a child travelling on foot — 100 = friendliest catchment in Singapore, 0 = least. It rates the walking environment, not how close amenities are, and is comparative across the 160 schools rather than an absolute pass/fail.

WHAT IT CAPTURES

The child's walk

A high score means short, legible routes (few confusing turns), dense controlled crossings, leafy shaded streets and continuous footpaths — the conditions under which a parent lets a child walk or cycle to school unaccompanied.

WHY IT MATTERS IN SG

Policy-relevant by design

Singapore's MOE gives admission priority within 1–2 km of a school, so a large share of pupils live in genuine walking range. The score shows where that walking environment actually delivers — directly informing active-travel, road-safety and the “car-lite” agenda.

CONTEXT

Tropical & safety lens

In Singapore's climate, greenery (the heaviest-weighted indicator, 0.26) is shade and heat relief, not just amenity; crossings & signals are the front line of child road safety; space-syntax legibility means routes a young child can navigate. That is why these dominate the index.

HOW TO USE IT

Targeting, not judgement

A Low score is not a “bad school” — it flags a catchment where greening, crossing upgrades or footpath continuity would most improve children's walk. Bottom-decile catchments are the priority list for intervention.

Why the pattern inverts 15-minute access. Friendly catchments cluster in newer towns (Jurong West, Punggol) built with wide green park-connector footpaths and regular signalised crossings; the dense central core (Bukit Timah, Newton) scores lower because mature, fine-grained street networks have more turns, less green corridor and busier roads — even though shops sit closer. The score is measuring a different thing than proximity: the quality of the journey, which is what determines whether a child walks.

Methodology

Faithful to the source paper's chain — schools → walking network → space syntax → catchment → entropy index → Geographic Detector — adapted to Singapore and computed end-to-end on Propheus infrastructure. The logic: define the real walkable area a child crosses to reach school, measure its environmental quality from objective open data, fuse those measures into one score with no subjective weighting, then statistically test which contextual factors explain the resulting pattern. Where Lanzhou used the Amap routing API and Baidu street view, Singapore substitutes the OpenStreetMap pedestrian graph and the Digital Atlas v4 layers; the street-view dimension is deferred to Phase 2.

STEP 1 · CATCHMENT

1 km network catchment

For each school, the streets reachable within 1 km network distance (not Euclidean) on the OSM walk graph — the MOE home-school registration priority band. The corridor = reachable edges buffered 40 m.

STEP 2 · SPACE SYNTAX

Angular Integration & Choice

cityseer angular analysis on the dual graph (235,600 nodes). Harmonic closeness → Integration (reachability); betweenness → Choice (through-movement), at 800 m & 1600 m radii.

STEP 3 · SAFETY & GREENERY

Objective environment

Controlled crossings & traffic signals per km of route (safety); park/green area as a share of the catchment (greenery); walkable footpath density (provision). All clipped to the catchment corridor.

STEP 4 · INDEX

Entropy-weighted composite

Six indicators min-max normalised, then combined by the entropy weight method — weights derived from each indicator's information content, no subjective tuning — and rescaled 0–100.

Formulae

Entropy weight of indicator j over n catchments:

pⱼ = rᵢⱼ / Σᵢ rᵢⱼ   ·   eⱼ = −(1/ln n) Σᵢ pᵢⱼ ln pᵢⱼ   ·   wⱼ = (1−eⱼ) / Σⱼ(1−eⱼ)

Friendliness, and the Geographic Detector power statistic q (h = driver strata, 5 quantiles):

Friendliness = Σⱼ wⱼ rᵢⱼ   ·   q = 1 − ( Σₕ Nₕ σ²ₕ ) / ( N σ² )  ∈ [0,1]

Disjoint sets. Index indicators (route environment) and Geographic Detector drivers (population density, transport convenience, district size, school centrality) are kept strictly separate, so q is never circular — the central design rule of the analysis.

Scope — Phase 1. This measures network structure plus objective environment proxies. The source paper's street-view experiential layer (green-view index, sky/enclosure, sidewalk ratio from semantic segmentation of Google Street View / Mapillary) is Phase 2 and requires a GPU.

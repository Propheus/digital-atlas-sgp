City Fingerprints — contrastive embeddings of Singapore's regions & places

Training method & applications · plexis-e1 (1,191 neighbourhoods) + plexis-p1 (190,591 places) · Digital Atlas v5.4.0 · 2026-06-13

1 · Abstract

We turned every neighbourhood and every place in Singapore into a fingerprint — a short list of numbers where distance means functional similarity. Two places (or two neighbourhoods) whose fingerprints sit close together play the same role in the city, even if they are on opposite ends of the island.

The fingerprints were learned by contrastive training: a matching game played millions of times. The network must recognise two distorted copies of the same unit as "the same", recognise two outlets of the same chain as siblings, and agree with a second network that reads only the unit's surroundings. Nobody ever defines "similar" — it emerges from the game.

Everything was proven the hard way: every test was written and frozen before training began, including one test the model must fail on purpose (predicting star ratings from a fingerprint that was promised to be popularity-free). The exam decided what shipped — and it rejected the best-scoring model once.

2 · Design

The substrate

Both models stand on the Digital Atlas v5 feature base: 801 validated measurements per hex8 neighbourhood (population, land use, buildings, roads, transit, places, business records, rents, flows — 64/64 layer gates passed) and 190,591 places, each with a micrograph of its 400/800 m surroundings.

plexis-e1 — the neighbourhood fingerprint

1,191 hex8 cells → 256 numbers each.

  • Input: 739 prepared features in five views — WHO (people), WHERE (built form), WHAT (places), FLOW (movement), PRICE.
  • Training: SCARF-style corruption (mask 30% of features, the two views must still match) plus view-masking, InfoNCE loss.
  • The twist: the pure neural model scored best on prediction probes but quietly pulled opposite districts (Tuas ↔ Orchard) together — a locked check caught it. Shipped = hybrid: 160 classical (PCA) dimensions preserve global geography + 96 contrastive dimensions sharpen local character.

plexis-p1 — the place fingerprint

190,591 places → 64 numbers each. Purely structural:

  • What it is — category, chain/independent, brand size.
  • What surrounds it — the 18-feature micrograph: rivals at 400/800 m, complements, anchors, walk-to-MRT.
  • Where it sits — pressed in during training via a second tower that reads the hex's frozen e1 vector + the 400 m category mix. The place never stores its location; it learns to encode what kind of context it fits.
  • Hard rule: no ratings, no reviews, no popularity — anywhere. Audited (§3).

The three training signals (p1)

  1. Recognise yourself — two corrupted copies of the same place must match (SCARF, mask 0.3).
  2. Recognise your siblings — 17,046 same-brand outlet pairs from 203 staffed chains (ATMs, vending, EV chargers denylisted; 20% of every chain hidden for the exam).
  3. Agree with your street — the place tower and the context tower must produce matching vectors for the same place (CLIP-style cross-view).

Hard negatives by construction: half of every batch is same-category groups, so "all cafes are alike" is a losing strategy. Training cost: e1 full program 8 minutes; p1 52 seconds per seed (full 190K) — CPU only.

The validation-first protocol

For each model, a battery of falsifiable checks — thresholds included — was frozen before any training: known-twin and known-contrast panels, held-out chain retrieval, geography-leak caps, probes with spatially-blocked cross-validation, negative controls, seed stability, and the forbidden probe. The harness, not the loss curve, makes the ship/no-ship call. Usage rule for both: use the vectors RAW — never re-standardise per dimension (re-scaling amplifies near-constant dimensions and destroys the validated geometry).

4 · Applications

With the neighbourhood fingerprint (hex8 · e1)

Pilot here, roll out there

When you trial something new — a covered walkway, a feeder bus, a community programme — in one neighbourhood, the real question is "where else will this work?" The honest answer is not "the places nearby" but "the places that function the same way." The fingerprint hands you that list at once: trial it in Toa Payoh Central and the rollout candidates are Tiong Bahru, Townsville and Bendemeer — neighbourhoods that share its density, its MRT footfall and its street life, even though they sit scattered across the island. Your findings travel along similarity, not along the train line.

Fair benchmarking

A rent, a business-closure rate, a footfall count means little on its own. Is $6 a square foot high? It depends entirely on what kind of place you are standing in. The fingerprint lets you compare a neighbourhood only to its five functional twins — places genuinely like it — so "30% more expensive than places just like it" becomes a real, defensible statement instead of an apples-to-oranges league table.

Gap detection — what's missing here?

If five neighbourhoods are functionally identical and four of them support a supermarket, a clinic and three tuition centres — but the fifth doesn't — that fifth one isn't merely different, it's underserved. The twins quietly tell you what a place like this should have; the gap between that expectation and reality is a ready-made shortlist of opportunities, found without anyone guessing.

New-town trajectory

A growing estate like Tengah is still becoming something. Re-run the fingerprints each year and you can watch which established town Tengah is drifting toward in function space — today it leans toward Bukit Batok and Yishun. That tells you which town's amenity build-out, school pressure and commercial mix to expect next, using a real place's decade of history as the forecast.

Model fuel

Any data model the team builds — for demand, risk, accessibility — normally has to juggle 801 raw columns. The 256-number fingerprint carries the city's structure in compact form: on held-out tests it rebuilds housing prices (0.81), transit throughput (0.90) and the adequacy score (0.93) on its own. So every future model starts richer and simpler, with the city already encoded.

With the place fingerprint (p1)

Expansion scouting

Your best-performing outlet sits in a particular kind of spot. "Find me twenty more spots like it" used to mean gut feeling and a wall map. Now it's one query: the twelve nearest fingerprints are the same kind of venue in the same kind of setting, ranked by genuine structural similarity rather than by who collected the most five-star reviews.

Brand siting DNA

Average the fingerprints of all of a chain's existing outlets and you get its siting DNA — a single number-portrait of the kind of corner that brand thrives on. Score every neighbourhood against that DNA and the top of the list is a ghost map of where the next outlets belong. When we ran this for FairPrice, the map pointed at Yunnan — the very neighbourhood a separate government study had flagged as a supermarket desert — which the model had never seen.

Cast this corner

Flip the question around: point at an empty shop unit and ask "what belongs here?" The fingerprint reads the corner's surroundings and retrieves the kinds of business that survive in corners like it elsewhere in the city — "this spot wants a clinic, a bakery, a tuition centre" — with the lookalike corners attached as evidence rather than a hunch.

Misfit detection — early risk warning

Most places fit their street; a few sit oddly far from everything around them. Sometimes that's a bold contrarian bet, but more often it's a tenant in the wrong spot. Flagging these misfits and cross-checking them against the atlas's record of where businesses go to die gives lenders, landlords and operators an early warning before the closure happens.

True market segments

The word "cafe" lumps together a void-deck kopitiam, a specialty roaster and a mall chain that share nothing but a label. The fingerprints sort all 190,591 places into 48 genuinely functional groups, so market sizing, competitor sets and white-space analysis run on what places actually are — not on the misleading category tag they happen to carry.
